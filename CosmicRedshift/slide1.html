Cosmological Redshift (Definition)
Cosmological redshift is the stretching of a light wave's wavelength caused by the expansion of space itself while the light is in transit. It is not caused by the source moving through space.
λobs = λemit × (1 + z)
Observed wavelength = emitted wavelength × (1 + redshift)  |  z = 0 means no stretching

Picture a wave drawn on a rubber sheet. Grab two edges of the sheet and pull. The wave you drew does not move along the sheet — but every crest and trough gets farther apart. Its wavelength grows because the sheet underneath it grew.

That is exactly what happens to light crossing the expanding Universe. The star does not have to be moving at all. Space between the star and the observer expands during the light's journey, and the wave is stretched along with it.

💡 Common confusion: This is not the Doppler effect. A Doppler shift comes from a source moving through space toward or away from you — the siren on a passing ambulance. Cosmological redshift comes from space itself expanding. The two look similar in a spectrum but have completely different physical causes.

Reading the Redshift Number z
The redshift z tells you the fractional increase in wavelength. Add 1 to it and you get the stretch factor directly.

A few worked values for our 450 nm blue photon:

z = 0.5 → stretch factor 1.5 → 675 nm (deep red, still visible)
z = 0.67 → stretch factor 1.67 → 750 nm (leaving the visible band)
z = 1 → stretch factor 2 → 900 nm (near-infrared)
z = 10.3 → stretch factor 11.3 → 5085 nm ≈ 5.1 µm (mid-infrared)

The final line is the one to remember. It is the light we will follow through the rest of this module — and it is why the earliest galaxies can only be photographed with an infrared telescope such as JWST, never with an optical one.
