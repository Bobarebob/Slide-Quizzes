The Pulse We Are Following
A single monochromatic pulse of 450 nm blue light leaving a hot B-type blue star roughly 450 million years after the Big Bang, at a redshift of z = 10.3.

That pulse then travels for 13.3 billion years before it reaches Earth. The Universe is 13.8 billion years old today, so this light has been in flight for almost the entire lifetime of the Universe.

📏 Distance and time are the same number here. The animation's distance meter is marked in billions of light-years (Gly). Because the pulse travels at the speed of light, a reading of 6.3 Gly of distance is also 6.3 billion years of travel time. One scale, two meanings.

The timeline in the animation is linear in distance. What is not linear is the wavelength — it creeps for billions of years and then runs away.

Fig. 3 — Slow at first, then explosive: the wavelength curve is strongly non-linear
The Crossover Point
The pulse stops being visible light at 750 nm, which happens at a redshift of only z ≈ 0.67 — about 6.3 billion light-years into its 13.3 billion light-year journey.

Reckoned against the age of the Universe, the crossover happens about 6.8 billion years after the Big Bang — which is roughly 7 billion years ago. For the whole second half of its trip, this pulse has been invisible.

It arrives at Earth at a little over 5 microns, or 5000 nanometers: mid-infrared. You cannot see it. A thermal camera or an infrared space telescope can.

🎨 About the brown color in the animation: once the pulse crosses into the infrared there is no honest color left to draw it in, because there is no color your eye would assign to it. The animation false-colors it as a dull brown that darkens and dims as the wavelength stretches further. That fading is a deliberate visual cue, not a physical property of the light.
