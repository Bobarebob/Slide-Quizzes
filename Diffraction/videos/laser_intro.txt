Today we will perform a lab on diffraction.
Recall that diffraction is the bending of light around corners.
We say that light is diffracted, or bent, not because it is going from one optical or transparent medium to another, but because it is encountering an edge or corner.
The reason light bends around corners is that light is a wave.
So when a light wave encounters an edge, it obeys Huygens' principle that states that each point on a wave's front can be considered a point source of circular wavelets.
So when a light wave encounters an edge, it naturally migrates around that edge in a circular fashion.
This is why shadows under tables are fuzzy, not sharply defined.
Diffraction is amplified when coherent light passes through two narrow slits.
Laser light is coherent light, meaning that all of its rays are of one frequency, and all of its rays travel in one direction.
If the difference in the path lengths of two wavelets, coming out of two narrow slits and traveling to a faraway wall, equals one or more wavelengths of a laser's light, the two wavelets of light combine to be a bright spot.
For a diffraction grating, basically many slits.
The path differences of the two wavelets must be exactly one or more wavelengths, otherwise there will be no bright spot at all.
In the animation that follows, we will look at just two dots coming out of a diffraction grating for each of three lasers, where the two dots land on a relatively far away wall.
By knowing the distance between adjacent slits in the diffraction grating and measuring the angle between a specific laser's central dot and its one bright off-axis dot, we will be able to calculate the wavelength of that laser's light and compare it to the wavelength marked on the laser itself.
We will do this for three different lasers, one red, one green, and one blue.
