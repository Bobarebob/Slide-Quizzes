Congratulations. You have finished Planetary Retrograde Motion.

Let's summarize what you learned.

You started with a problem. Planets sometimes appear to reverse
direction against the background stars. The Earth-centered model could
reproduce that motion, but only by stacking circles within circles,
called epicycles and deferents, one set for every planet. It worked, and it was
enormously complicated.

Copernicus moved the Sun to the center and the problem dissolved.
Retrograde motion is not something the planets do. It is something we
see, because we are observing from a moving platform. No epicycles
required. The simpler explanation turned out to be the correct one, and
that is Occam's Principle at work.

Then you watched the geometry in two different cases.

Mars is an outer planet, and it moves more slowly than Earth. Earth
takes one year to orbit the Sun; Mars takes one point eight eight
years. Because Earth travels the inside track, it periodically catches
up to Mars and passes it. During that pass, Mars appears to slide
backward against the faraway stars. That happens at opposition, when Earth sits
directly between Mars and the Sun.

Venus is an inner planet, and the situation reverses. Venus completes an
orbit in zero point six two years, so Venus laps us. Its retrograde
motion occurs at inferior conjunction, when Venus passes between Earth
and the Sun.

Same illusion, opposite geometry. In both cases the planet never stops
moving forward around the Sun. Only our line of sight changes.
