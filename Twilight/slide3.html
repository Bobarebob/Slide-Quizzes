London in June
The astronomical timer stays blank because the Sun never gets more than about 15° below the horizon. It needs 18° to bring on astronomical darkness and never makes it, so there is no fully dark sky in London for weeks around midsummer. The sky at one in the morning is deep blue, not black.
Fort Yukon in June
Every box is blank because there is no sunset. The Sun circles the sky without crossing the horizon — the midnight Sun. Switch the viewpoint from West to Northwest and you can see why: the Sun swings around to the north instead of dropping. Looking west, you were watching the wrong part of the sky.
💡 Where each stage gives out. On the summer solstice, astronomical twilight disappears above 48.6°, nautical above 54.6°, civil above 60.6°, and the Sun stops setting altogether above 66.6° — the Arctic Circle.
❄️ Then try the Winter Solstice at Fort Yukon, with the viewpoint on Southwest. Now the Sun never rises — but the nautical and astronomical timers show real numbers, and around the middle of the day the sky visibly brightens. Polar night is not a solid block of darkness. It is a long twilight that almost, but never quite, becomes day.
